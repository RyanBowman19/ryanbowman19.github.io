# 03 — Copy Deck

**Voice:** plain, direct, spoken. Short sentences. No throat-clearing.
**Reading level:** 6th grade. Every sentence is one a person would say out loud.
**Audience:** owner-operators of local Indiana businesses — trades, shops, food,
services, civic. Busy. Skeptical. Probably burned once. Reading on a phone.
**Goal:** one action — start a free site review.

---

## The rules this copy follows

**Banned words.** delve · elevate · seamless · cutting-edge · bespoke · tapestry ·
unleash · unlock · transformative · beacon · premier · bustling · paramount ·
dive in. Zero instances on the page — verified with grep, not by eye.

Also cut: "solutions," "digital presence," "leverage," "empower," "robust,"
"streamline." If it would sound odd said out loud at a parts counter, it's out.

**Hard limits.**
- H1: 7 words max → **6** ("Get found. Get called. Get hired.")
- Hero subhead: 2 sentences max → **2**
- Any paragraph: 3 sentences max
- Active voice only
- "You" and "your," never "our clients"

**Rhythm.** Vary the length. Short sentence to land the point. Then a longer one
that explains it. Then short again. The page should read like someone talking,
not like something written.

**No invented facts.** No made-up statistics. No fake reviews. No performance
numbers that haven't been measured. Your market is small enough that people
check, and being the honest one is the whole differentiator.

---

## The 8-second scan test

Squint at the page. In eight seconds a visitor gets:

| # | Element | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Get found. Get called. Get hired.** | What is this? |
| 2 | *People search for what you do every day…* | Is it for me? |
| 3 | Amber button — **Get my free site review** | What do I do? |
| 4 | One price · You own it all · Built in Indiana | Can I trust it? |

One amber element in the first screen. It's the button.

## Hero

> **Get found. Get called. Get hired.**
>
> People search for what you do every day. If you're not online, they call
> someone else.
>
> `[Get my free site review →]`  `[See one I built]`
>
> ✓ One price, up front ✓ You own it all ✓ Built in Indiana

**Why this H1.** Three verbs, three results, in the order they happen. It's
about what the customer gets, not what you make. Compare to the version this
replaced — "Websites that turn searches into customers" — which is accurate,
longer, and reads like a marketing department wrote it.

**Why the subhead works.** Sentence one is a fact they already believe.
Sentence two is the consequence they hadn't put words to. Two sentences, no
adjectives, and the second one stings a little.

**CTA wording.** "Get **my** free site review." *My* makes it theirs. *Free*
kills the risk. *Review* promises a thing, not a sales call. Never "Submit" or
"Learn more" — both describe your action, not their gain.

## Problem

> **No website? You're losing work.**
>
> Here's what happens when someone looks you up and finds nothing.

| Card | Copy |
|---|---|
| **They can't find you** | Someone types your trade and your town into Google. Three shops come up. You're not one of them. |
| **Your site fights their phone** | Most folks check you on a phone. If they have to pinch and zoom to find your number, they quit. Then they call the next guy. |
| **An old site looks closed** | A dead phone number. A copyright date from 2016. People read that as "out of business" and move on. |

> None of this is hard to fix. It just has to get done.

**Note the fragments.** "A dead phone number." isn't a complete sentence. That's
on purpose — it's how people talk when they're listing things off, and it breaks
up the rhythm so the paragraph doesn't drone.

**No statistics anywhere.** The temptation is "75% of consumers judge
credibility by website design." It's a real-sounding number that traces back to
nothing solid. Concrete scenes persuade better and can't be fact-checked against
you.

## Benefits

> **A site that actually brings you work**
>
> Not a brochure nobody reads. A tool that answers the four things people want
> to know, then makes it easy to call you.

| Benefit | Copy |
|---|---|
| Your number, one tap away | On every page. They tap it, their phone dials. That's it. |
| Loads fast on bad service | Works out in the county on two bars. No spinning wheel. |
| Hours, map, and prices up top | The stuff people actually came for. No hunting. |
| Your Google listing, done right | This is what really drives "near me" searches. I set it up. |
| The words written for you | I ask questions. You talk. I write it. You say yes. |
| A person to call | Not a ticket number. Not a chatbot. Me. |

Every one is what they **get**, not what you do. "Responsive design" is a
feature; "works out in the county on two bars" is a benefit, and it's the same
thing said by someone who's been out in the county.

"Not a ticket number. Not a chatbot. Me." is the shortest line on the page and
probably the most persuasive. It's the one thing a big agency can't say.

- **Yours needs work** *(Most asked for)* — *It works, but it looks ten years old.*

finds themselves in one glance instead of reading four bullets to learn the card
isn't about them.

## Testimonials — ⚠ PLACEHOLDER

The three quotes on the page are **examples of shape, not real reviews.**
Attribution reads `[Client name]` / `[Business], [Town], IN` so it can't ship by
accident, and there's a visible note under the section.

What makes the example quotes work, so real ones can be chosen the same way:

1. **They name a specific outcome.** "A guy called and said he found us on his
   phone at a job site." Not "great service!"
2. **They admit a doubt first.** "I was scared it'd be complicated." A quote
   that starts with hesitation and ends satisfied handles an objection for you.
3. **They mention the price holding.** "Price was exactly what he quoted." The
   #1 fear in this trade, answered by somebody who isn't you.

**Getting real ones:** ask right after launch, while they're pleased. Ask a
specific question — *"What were you worried about before we started?"* — not
"can I get a testimonial." You'll get usable words instead of "very
professional, highly recommend." Get written permission to use their name and
business.

If you have none yet, **delete the section.** An empty spot is fine. A made-up
review is not.

## Work

> **See one I built** — A library serves everyone. Old phones, slow rural
> service, screen readers. So the site puts the catalog, the events, and the
> phone number right where people look.

Claims kept to what a visitor can verify by clicking through: catalog search on
the front page, phone reachable throughout, published accessibility statement,
staff-editable events.

The mockup mirrors the real homepage — same hero line, same catalog search, same
phone number. **Do not draw a mockup of the site you wish you'd built.** An
earlier draft showed "Open today until 6:00," which is a recommendation from
`06-ux-audit.md`, not something on the live site. That's the kind of small lie
that costs you a client who clicks the link.

## Process

> **Four steps. No mystery.**

| # | Title | Copy | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | We talk | Twenty minutes. I look at what you've got. If you don't need me, I'll tell you. | Day 1 |
| 2 | You get a price | One page. What I'll build, what it costs, when it's done. That number doesn't move. | Within 2 days |
| 3 | I build it | You watch it happen on a private link. Not a slideshow — the real thing. Two rounds of changes included. | 1–3 weeks |
| 4 | You go live | I handle the domain, the hosting, and Google. Then I stick around. | Launch day |

The timing column is the highest-value thing in this section. "How long, and
when do you need me?" is the anxiety under every small-business web project, and
almost nobody answers it on the page.

## Estimate

> **What will this cost?** — Slide the controls. Watch the number. No email
> needed — I'd rather you know before we talk.
>
> `[Get a real price]`
> *This is a ballpark. Your real price comes after a free 20-minute call —
> usually it's less.*

Showing price is the biggest call on the page. Standard practice hides it to
force contact. That backfires here — this audience reads a hidden price as "if
you have to ask." You lose tire-kickers, which is the point, and everyone left
arrives already anchored.

"Usually it's less" is a small, believable reversal that leaves room for good
news at quote time. Only say it if it's true.

## Care plans

> **Or don't. Your call.** — These are optional. The site is yours either way.

An H2 that tells people they can skip the upsell. It defuses the fear that the
monthly plan is the trap, which is exactly what makes the middle tier sell.

## FAQ

Ordered by how early the question forms, not by how comfortable it is to answer.

1. What does a website cost?
2. How long does it take?
3. I don't have photos or know what to write.
4. **Do I own it, or am I renting?**
5. Can I change it myself?
6. Will Google find me?
7. I kind of like my site.
8. Where are you?

**#4 is the one that closes deals.** A lot of small businesses have been through
a "web guy" who held the domain hostage. Naming it — *"No holding your site
hostage. Some folks in this business do that. I won't."* — beats any feature
list on the page.

**#6 ends with "Anyone who promises you a spot is lying to you."** You'll lose
the prospect who wants a guarantee. That prospect was going to be miserable.

**#7 talks them out of the expensive option.** Which is why it's the most
persuasive paragraph here.

## Hierarchy and spacing

- **One `<h1>`.** Sections are `<h2>`, cards `<h3>`. Never skip a level — screen
  reader users navigate by heading and a gap reads as a missing section.
- **Bullets any time there are 3+ parallel items.** Nobody reads a paragraph of
  four things on a phone.
- **No paragraph over 3 sentences.** Enforced everywhere on the page.
- **Line length capped around 65 characters.** One CSS property, biggest
  readability win available.
- **Space scales with importance.** The hero gets the most room; FAQ rows the
  least. That's how you signal what matters without making anything bigger.
- **Sections alternate** paper / tinted / paper, so the eye gets a boundary
  without a divider line.
